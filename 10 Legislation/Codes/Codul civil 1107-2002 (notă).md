# Codul civil nr. 1107/2002 — Master analysis note

**Raw text index:** [[Codul civil 1107-2002 (text) — Index]] ✅ *complete, split by Books* · **Hub:** [[MOC — Drept civil]] · **Procedure counterpart:** [[Codul administrativ 116-2018 (notă)]]

> [!success] Complete text, split because of size
> The civil code is too large for a single useful vault file. The text is therefore split into five book files and indexed at [[Codul civil 1107-2002 (text) — Index]]. Use this note as the master map; use the book files for article-level work.

## 1. What it is

The Civil Code is the general private-law code of Moldova. It governs:

- persons and legal capacity;
- legal acts and representation;
- limitation periods;
- property and other real rights;
- obligations and contracts;
- succession;
- private international law.

For commercial/legal work, it is the default law unless displaced by a special statute.

Energy example: a power-purchase agreement, connection contract or supply contract may sit inside a dense public-law framework, but its basic contract-law questions still run through Book III unless the energy statute or HANRE act supplies a special rule.

## 3. The 2018–2019 reform

The Civil Code should not be treated as a static 2002 text. The major reform effective around 2019 reshaped Moldovan private law:

- terminology and structure were modernised;
- professional/consumer distinctions became central;
- contract and obligations rules were heavily revised;
- the code moved closer to modern European private-law models;
- multiple special laws were absorbed or harmonised into the code architecture.

This matters when reading older commentary or old contracts. A pre-2019 clause may still be valid, but the doctrinal vocabulary used to analyse it may now be different.

## 4. Monist model

Moldova uses a **monist civil-code model**: the Civil Code is the general private-law code for both ordinary civil relationships and professional/commercial relationships.

There is no separate commercial code doing the work that a commercial code does in some civil-law systems. Instead, the Civil Code distinguishes **consumer** and **professionist** status.

Art. 3 of Book I defines:

- **consumer** — a natural person acting predominantly outside business/professional purposes;
- **professionist** — a person acting for business/professional purposes, including non-profit professional activity;
- mixed-status cases are resolved by whether recognising consumer status offers legal protection.

That distinction is now a major gateway into mandatory-protection rules.

## 5. Book I — general provisions

Book I begins with the principles of civil legislation:

- equality of participants;
- private and family life protection;
- inviolability of property;
- freedom of contract;
- good faith;
- consumer protection;
- non-interference in private affairs;
- free exercise and judicial defence of civil rights.

Art. 4 says subordinate normative acts apply to civil relationships only when issued under law and not contrary to law. That is important in regulated sectors: a HANRE act can shape a contract only within its statutory delegation.

Book I also contains legal acts, representation, terms, limitation and status rules for persons.

## 6. Book II — real rights

Book II covers property and other real rights.

Use it for:

- ownership;
- possession;
- accession and fixtures;
- servitudes and other limited real rights;
- security interests;
- land/building questions;
- property consequences of infrastructure works.

Energy connection: network infrastructure, protection zones, access rights and applicant-funded works often produce property-law questions. The specific energy statute gives the public-law power; Book II supplies the private-law grammar.

## 7. Book III — why it is the workhorse

Book III is the workhorse for commercial practice. It contains:

- general obligations;
- performance;
- non-performance remedies;
- contract formation and interpretation;
- named contracts;
- security and guarantees;
- unjust enrichment and delict/tort-type obligations.

It starts at art. 774. The opening provisions matter:

- art. 774 — general provision on obligations;
- art. 775 — good faith in obligations;
- art. 776 — grounds for obligations.

For almost every energy/private-law file, Book III is where the contract analysis begins.

## 8. Performance and non-performance

Key Book III anchors:

- art. 858 — general conditions for performance;
- arts. 901–946 — remedies for non-performance;
- art. 903 — circumstances justifying non-performance;
- art. 904 — impediment/justification;
- arts. 915–923 — termination (`rezoluțiune`) for non-performance;
- arts. 934–946 — damages, foreseeability, mitigation, interest and currency.

These are the articles to check when a regulated contract fails:

- energy not delivered;
- connection works delayed;
- balancing obligations breached;
- procurement contract not performed;
- supply interrupted outside the permitted regulatory grounds.

Public-law permission to interrupt service and private-law liability for breach are related but not identical questions.

## 9. Contract formation

Book III contract anchors:

- art. 992 — notion of contract;
- art. 993 — freedom of contract;
- art. 996 — binding force;
- art. 997 — obligation to contract;
- art. 1003 — framework contract;
- arts. 1025–1026 — bad-faith negotiation and confidentiality;
- art. 1027 — agreement on essential terms;
- art. 1028 — form;
- arts. 1029–1044 — offer, acceptance and conclusion mechanics.

Regulated sectors frequently limit freedom of contract. The correct framing is not "Civil Code versus regulation"; it is Civil Code default rules plus mandatory special rules from the sectoral statute or HANRE act.

## 10. Named contracts

Book III then moves through named contracts: sale, lease, works/services, mandate, agency-like relationships, insurance, credit, surety/guarantees and other contract types.

For energy and infrastructure work, the most common analogies are:

- sale/supply of energy as a special regulated sale/supply relationship;
- works contracts for connection and network development;
- services contracts for operation and maintenance;
- surety/guarantee rules for performance-security instruments;
- framework contracts for repeated market trades.

Always check the special energy act first for mandatory deviations.

## 11. Book IV — succession

Book IV governs succession. It is usually outside energy/regulatory work, but it can matter for:

- inherited real estate connected to heat/gas/electricity networks;
- transfer of shares/participations in family businesses;
- inheritance of claims or debts.

The index shows Book IV includes 414 articles.

## 12. Book V — private international law

Book V governs conflict-of-laws rules. Use it when:

- a contract has a foreign governing-law clause;
- parties are in different jurisdictions;
- assets or performance are cross-border;
- recognition/enforcement or applicable law is disputed.

Energy cross-border contracts may require both Book V and sector-specific public-law analysis.

## 13. Relationship to administrative law

The Civil Code does not govern administrative procedure. If the disputed act is an ANRE licence decision, tariff decision, sanction, connection-refusal review or procurement-remedy decision, start with [[Codul administrativ 116-2018 (notă)]].

But private-law consequences may remain:

- damages;
- contract termination;
- restitution;
- payment obligations;
- guarantees.

The hard cases are mixed: public-law decision upstream, civil-law contract downstream.

## 14. Relationship to sectoral procurement

Procurement contracts are awarded through public/sectoral procurement law, but performance and modification may also raise civil-law questions.

Read with [[Legea 74-2020 — achizitii sectoriale (notă)]] for utilities procurement, especially energy.

## 15. How to use this master note

1. Start at the index.
2. Choose the relevant Book.
3. Use the Civil Code for defaults.
4. Check special statutes for mandatory deviations.
5. For public authority decisions, switch to the Administrative Code.

## 16. Practitioner cautions

- Do not rely on pre-2019 civil-law commentary without checking whether the article survived reform.
- Do not assume contract freedom overrides regulated-sector mandatory rules.
- In consumer/professionist cases, art. 3 can change the entire analysis.
- In energy contracts, always identify whether the issue is public-law, private-law or mixed.
- Because the code is split across files, article links should target the book file, not the index.

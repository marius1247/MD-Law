# Legea nr. 92/2014 cu privire la energia termică și promovarea cogenerării — Analysis

## 1. What it is

This is the sectoral law for **district heating and cogeneration**. It regulates centralised heat supply systems (`sisteme de alimentare centralizată cu energie termică`) and promotes high-efficiency cogeneration.

It is the heat-sector equivalent of:

- [[Legea 164-2025 — energia electrica (notă)|L164/2025]] for electricity;
- [[Legea 108-2016 — gazele naturale (notă)|L108/2016]] for gas;
- [[Legea 10-2016 — surse regenerabile (notă)|L10/2016]] for renewables support.

But its economic problem is different. Heat networks are local, seasonal, infrastructure-heavy and socially sensitive. The law therefore reads less like a market-opening statute and more like a public-service statute with regulated tariffs and consumer-protection rules.

## 2. Scope and public-service character

Art. 1 defines the object: activities specific to centralised heat supply systems, aimed at improving energy efficiency and reducing environmental impact, including by using cogeneration.

Art. 2(2) is the key clause: production, distribution and supply of heat through a centralised system are **public services of general interest**.

That phrase should shape interpretation. Heat supply is not treated as ordinary commodity trading. The law assumes:

- continuity;
- affordability;
- consumer protection;
- tariff regulation;
- network access;
- security and reliability of service.

## 4. Definitions to pin

Art. 5 supplies the vocabulary:

- **cogenerare** — simultaneous production of heat and electricity/mechanical energy in the same process;
- **cogenerare de înaltă eficiență** — cogeneration meeting the criteria in annex no. 1;
- **centrală electrică de termoficare** — combined heat-and-power plant;
- **distribuitor**, **furnizor**, **producător** — heat-sector actors;
- **consumator casnic** and **consumator noncasnic** — household/non-household consumers;
- **sistem de alimentare centralizată cu energie termică** — the centralised heat supply system;
- **unitate termoenergetică** — the undertaking carrying out heat activities.

The definition set is narrower than L164/2025. There is no equivalent market-design vocabulary for aggregation, trading, OPEED or REMIT. The sector remains organised around infrastructure, service and tariff.

## 5. ANRE's role

Art. 9 assigns [[ANRE]] a long list of sector-specific responsibilities. Read it with [[Legea 174-2017 — energetica (notă)]], which supplies ANRE's institutional status and independence.

In heat, ANRE's practical role includes:

- licensing production, distribution and supply activities → [[Concept — Licență în energetică]];
- approving regulated tariffs → [[Concept — Tarif reglementat]];
- approving methodologies and regulations;
- monitoring compliance by thermo-energy undertakings;
- protecting consumers within the regulated service framework;
- dispute handling and control.

The same cascade applies:

L92/2014 → ANRE methodology/regulation → individual tariff/licence/contractual position.

## 6. Cogeneration is the bridge to electricity

Cap. IV is the bridge between heat and electricity. Cogeneration is promoted because it uses fuel more efficiently than separate heat and electricity production.

Key provisions:

- art. 12 — analysis of national potential;
- art. 13 — support measures;
- art. 14 — guarantee of origin;
- art. 15 — electricity produced in cogeneration;
- arts. 15¹–15³ — later inserted rules around cogeneration/electricity treatment;
- art. 17 — statistics.

This is why [[HANRE 283-2020 — Regulile pietei energiei electrice (notă)|the electricity market rules]] still mention central urban cogeneration plants and the central electricity supplier. Heat demand can determine the operating profile of CHP plants, but the electricity produced then enters electricity-market allocation and regulated-price mechanisms.

## 7. Licensing

Cap. V, arts. 18–27, is the licensing chapter.

Art. 19 lists licence types. Art. 20 sets conditions and procedure. Art. 21 lists rights and obligations of licence-holders. Arts. 25–27 cover suspension, resumption and withdrawal.

For practice, separate three questions:

1. Is the activity within L92/2014's licensed perimeter?
2. Is the undertaking licensed for the relevant activity?
3. Does the contract/tariff/disconnection issue arise from licence obligations?

This note should be linked from [[Concept — Licență în energetică]] because heat-sector licensing has a public-service flavour that electricity trading does not.

## 8. Land, networks and protection zones

Cap. VI is practical infrastructure law:

- art. 28 — general principles on land use and rights connected to heat networks;
- arts. 28¹–28³ — later inserted detailed rights/conditions;
- art. 29 — restriction or suspension of activities affecting heat infrastructure;
- art. 30 — expropriation;
- art. 31 — protection zones of heat networks;
- art. 32 — merger, associations, separation/restructuring.

This is where heat law intersects property law and local government. Centralised heat systems are physically embedded in buildings, streets and municipal infrastructure. A consumer dispute may therefore have both contract and real-estate dimensions.

## 9. Supply and consumer relationships

Cap. VII and VIII are the operational heart:

- art. 34 — production of heat;
- art. 35 — purchase of heat produced in cogeneration;
- art. 36 — distribution and supply;
- art. 37 — consumer of heat;
- art. 38 — quality of distribution and supply services;
- art. 40 — heat-supply contract;
- art. 42 — interruption and limitation of heat supply;
- art. 43 — metering and payment;
- art. 44 — dispute settlement.

This is where L92/2014 differs from a pure market statute. The consumer contract, metering point and interruption rules are central because heat supply is a building-level and household-level service.

## 10. Tariff regulation

Art. 45 governs tariff regulation in the heat sector. Its policy logic is the same as in electricity and gas networks:

- regulated undertakings must recover justified costs;
- consumers must be protected against monopoly pricing;
- tariffs must be transparent and based on methodology;
- ANRE approves tariff methodology and applies it through individual tariff decisions.

Read art. 45 with [[Concept — Tarif reglementat]]. The regulated heat tariff is not a negotiated contractual price. It is a public-law price produced by statutory methodology and ANRE decision.

## 11. Why heat is politically sensitive

District heating is one of the most socially exposed parts of the energy system:

- household budgets are directly affected;
- service quality is visible immediately in winter;
- network losses and building inefficiency are hard to allocate fairly;
- cogeneration economics depend on both heat demand and electricity-market rules;
- municipal infrastructure and state policy overlap.

This is why the law emphasises affordability, public service, quality, continuity and tariff transparency.

## 13. Practitioner cautions

- Do not import electricity-market concepts into heat without checking L92/2014. Heat is more public-service/regulatory than competitive-market oriented.
- Cogeneration creates cross-sector issues: heat law may determine production constraints while electricity rules determine sale/allocation.
- Tariff disputes are public-law disputes even when they arise inside consumer bills.
- Local-authority roles matter more here than in national wholesale electricity trading.
- Check the current consolidation after LP227/2025, which is recorded in the text file as entering into force on 30.12.2025.

# Concept — Poziție dominantă

**RO:** poziție dominantă · **EN:** dominant position

## Definition
A position of economic strength on a relevant market that allows an undertaking to **behave to an appreciable extent independently** of competitors, customers and consumers. Governed by **Legea concurenței nr. 183/2012**, which follows **Article 102 TFEU** closely enough that EU case law is the working interpretive guide.

## The rule that gets misstated
**Dominance is not unlawful. Abuse of dominance is.** An undertaking may become dominant by being better, and is entitled to compete. What it may not do is use that position to foreclose rivals or exploit customers in ways a non-dominant firm could not.

Dominance does, however, carry a **special responsibility**: conduct that is unobjectionable from a small firm — an exclusivity clause, a loyalty rebate, a refusal to deal — can be an infringement when the same conduct comes from a dominant one.

## Establishing dominance — the sequence
1. **Define the relevant market**, in product and geographic terms. This is where cases are actually won and lost: a narrow market definition produces dominance, a wide one dissolves it. The standard tool is demand substitutability — would customers switch in response to a small but significant price rise.
2. **Measure market shares** — high and stable shares raise a presumption, but they are an indicator, not the test.
3. **Assess barriers to entry and expansion**, countervailing buyer power, and the competitive structure. A high share in a market that is easy to enter is worth little.

**Collective dominance** — two or more undertakings dominant together — is available where the market structure supports coordinated behaviour.

## Forms of abuse
| Type | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Exclusionary** | Predatory pricing, margin squeeze, exclusive dealing, loyalty rebates, tying, refusal to supply, denial of access to an essential facility |
| **Exploitative** | Excessive or unfair prices, unfair trading conditions, discrimination between equivalent transactions |

**Essential facilities and access** is where competition law and sector regulation meet. In network industries the access question is normally answered *ex ante* by the regulator — third-party access rules, network codes, tariffs — rather than *ex post* by the competition authority. → [[Energetică — sinteza sectorului]] · [[Concept — Unbundling]]

## Enforcement
The **[[Consiliul Concurenței]]** investigates and decides, with powers of inspection, interim measures, commitments and **fines calculated on turnover**. Decisions are subject to judicial review. Private damages actions are available in principle, though little used in practice.

## Why the regulated sectors are the interesting case
Dominance in Moldova is often **structural and lawful** — a single transmission operator, a single incumbent gas company. The regulatory response is not to prohibit the position but to constrain its exercise through licensing, unbundling, third-party access and regulated tariffs. Competition law then sits behind that as a backstop for conduct the sector rules do not reach.
